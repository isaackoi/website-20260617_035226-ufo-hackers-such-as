## Introduction

A central detail in Gary McKinnon’s “Non-Terrestrial Officers” story is his claim that the spreadsheet listed names and ranks of personnel who were “not registered anywhere else”. For some UFO researchers, that phrase has been treated as evidence that the individuals belonged to a hidden organisation operating outside normal military structures. However, the claim encounters an immediate practical problem: members of the public generally do not have comprehensive access to modern military personnel records, and the absence of a name from publicly searchable sources does not demonstrate that the person does not exist in official systems. The question is therefore not whether a civilian researcher could find the names, but whether there is evidence that the names were genuinely missing from military record systems. No such evidence has been publicly produced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He FoundJun 21, 2006 — One was titled &quot;Non-Terrestrial Officers.&quot; It contained names and ranks of U.S. Air F...</span></span></span>

## Were the Names Really Missing From Records?

The strongest version of the claim would require more than an inability to locate names through public searches. It would require proof that the individuals were absent from official personnel databases, service records, assignment records, payroll systems, or other internal military documentation.

Publicly, however, the story rests on McKinnon’s recollection of what he saw. In the interview most frequently cited by supporters of the claim, he stated that the spreadsheet contained names and ranks of US Air Force personnel “not registered anywhere else”. The underlying spreadsheet has never been released for independent examination, and no list of the alleged names has been made available for verification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He FoundJun 21, 2006 — One was titled &quot;Non-Terrestrial Officers.&quot; It contained names and ranks of U.S. Air F...</span></span></span>

Without the names themselves, researchers cannot test the assertion. They cannot compare the entries against military archives, personnel databases, veterans' records, unit rosters, or other administrative sources. The claim therefore remains a description of what McKinnon believed he observed rather than a finding that can be independently reproduced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He FoundJun 21, 2006 — One was titled &quot;Non-Terrestrial Officers.&quot; It contained names and ranks of U.S. Air F...</span></span></span>

## Military Record Access Rules

One reason the “unregistered names” argument is difficult to evaluate is that military personnel records are not generally open to unrestricted public inspection.

In the United States, Official Military Personnel Files remain subject to access restrictions for decades. The National Archives states that personnel records become fully open archival records only after a service member has been separated from service for 62 years. For more recent personnel, access is substantially limited and typically restricted to the veteran, authorised representatives, or next of kin under specific circumstances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/veterans/military-service-records" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">military service records</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRequest Military Service Records3 Mar 2026 — Military personnel records are open to the public 62 years after they leave...</span></span></span>

As a result, a researcher attempting to verify the existence of active-duty personnel, recently retired personnel, intelligence personnel, or individuals assigned to sensitive programmes would not normally have access to complete records. The inability to locate a name in publicly available material therefore has limited evidential value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/veterans/military-service-records" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">military service records</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRequest Military Service Records3 Mar 2026 — Military personnel records are open to the public 62 years after they leave...</span></span></span>

## Privacy Restrictions and Security Considerations

Military personnel information sits at the intersection of transparency and privacy. Freedom of Information laws provide mechanisms for requesting government records, but they also contain exemptions designed to protect personal information, operational security, and classified activities.

US government guidance explains that most information contained in modern personnel files is not freely releasable to the public without the consent of the individual concerned. Even when records are disclosed, sensitive details may be withheld or redacted. Defence agencies also apply exemptions relating to national security and privacy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/personnel-records-center/foia-info" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and The Privacy Act</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesFreedom of Information Act (FOIA) and The Privacy ActMay 29, 2025 — Most information in a Federal (non-archival) Officia...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2025</span></span></span>

This matters because advocates of the “unregistered names” interpretation sometimes assume that if a name cannot be found through public channels, the person must belong to a secret programme. In reality, many legitimate military personnel are difficult or impossible for members of the public to trace through open records alone. Privacy protections are a routine feature of personnel administration, not evidence of extraordinary status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/personnel-records-center/foia-info" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and The Privacy Act</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesFreedom of Information Act (FOIA) and The Privacy ActMay 29, 2025 — Most information in a Federal (non-archival) Officia...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2025</span></span></span>

## What Absence Actually Proves

The key analytical question is what conclusion can legitimately be drawn from a missing name.

Three possibilities are often conflated:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **The name is absent from public sources.**
2. **The name is absent from publicly obtainable military records.**<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.archives.gov/veterans/military-service-records" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">military service records</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRequest Military Service Records3 Mar 2026 — Military personnel records are open to the public 62 years after they leave...</span></span></span>
3. **The name is absent from all official military systems.**

</div>

Only the third possibility would strongly support the idea of a parallel organisation operating outside normal personnel administration. ## Why This Point Matters in the Non-Terrestrial Officers Debate

Within the broader debate over McKinnon’s “Non-Terrestrial Officers” spreadsheet, the “unregistered names” detail is often treated as one of the strongest elements of the story. Yet it is also one of the least verifiable.

The claim depends entirely on a reported observation rather than an accessible document. At the same time, the normal structure of military record-keeping means that outsiders cannot assume a person is fictitious merely because their records are not publicly visible. Modern personnel systems are not designed as open public registries, and access restrictions make comprehensive verification difficult or impossible for recent service members.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[wired.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He FoundJun 21, 2006 — One was titled &quot;Non-Terrestrial Officers.&quot; It contained names and ranks of U.S. Air F...</span></span></span>
