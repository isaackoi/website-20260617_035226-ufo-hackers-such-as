## Introduction

When assessing alleged UFO evidence connected to hackers such as Gary McKinnon, NASA’s 2023 study of Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP) offers an important lesson: unusual imagery or intriguing records are not meaningful on their own. The study concluded that many UAP investigations are limited not by a lack of dramatic claims, but by missing sensor [metadata]({{ 'metadata/' | relative_url }}), inadequate calibration records, insufficient baseline information, and the absence of multiple independent measurements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceIndependent Study Team ReportAt present, analysis of UAP data is hampered by poor sensor calibration, the lack of multiple me...</span></span></span>

## Why Sensor Metadata Matters

NASA’s independent UAP study repeatedly emphasised that sensor metadata is essential for interpreting unusual observations. The report noted that current analysis is often hampered by a lack of sensor metadata and that contextual information about the sensor is required to determine whether an anomaly is real, misinterpreted, or an artefact of the equipment itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceIndependent Study Team ReportAt present, analysis of UAP data is hampered by poor sensor calibration, the lack of multiple me...</span></span></span>

Metadata answers questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Which instrument captured the observation?
* What was the exact time and location?
* What settings were active?
* What processing occurred before the file was stored?
* What known limitations or error modes existed?

</div>

NASA specifically observed that some apparent UAP cases were later explained as sensor artefacts once calibration information and metadata were examined closely. In other words, what initially looked mysterious became understandable after investigators gained access to the [technical context]({{ 'technical-context/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/NASA_Unidentified_Anomalous_Phenomena%3A_Independent_Study_Team_Report/Responses_to_Statement_of_Task" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Responses to Statement of Task</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena: Independent...14 Oct 2023 — Indeed, several apparent UAP have been demonstrated to be s...</span></span></span>

For alleged hacker evidence, the same principle applies. Suppose a hacker claims to have viewed a NASA image showing an unknown object. Without the [original file]({{ 'original-file/' | relative_url }}), associated metadata, mission identifiers, instrument records, processing logs, and acquisition details, investigators cannot determine whether the image depicts an actual object, a sensor glitch, compression artefact, processing error, reflection, or something entirely mundane. The image alone is only part of the evidence.

## Calibration and Baseline Data Problems

One of the most important conclusions of NASA's UAP study was that poor sensor calibration and missing baseline data make anomaly assessment extremely difficult. The report argues that researchers need to know how instruments behave under ordinary conditions before they can confidently identify extraordinary conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceIndependent Study Team ReportAt present, analysis of UAP data is hampered by poor sensor calibration, the lack of multiple me...</span></span></span>

Baseline data are the normal reference points against which anomalies are judged. For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* What does a typical image from the instrument look like?
* How often do known artefacts appear?
* What processing steps are routinely applied?
* How frequently do false positives occur?

</div>

Without such references, investigators cannot tell whether an unusual feature is genuinely unusual.

This is particularly relevant to claims associated with hacked systems. A file extracted from a government or aerospace network may appear remarkable because outsiders lack knowledge of the routine workflows behind it. A strange label, image feature, or database field may be ordinary within the system's operational context. Without baseline examples from the same archive, analysts risk mistaking unfamiliarity for significance.

NASA's recommendation for systematic calibration and multiple measurements reflects a broader scientific principle: an anomaly becomes persuasive only when it survives comparison against known system behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/update-nasa-shares-uap-independent-study-report-names-director/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">update nasa shares uap independent study report names director</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report14 Sept 2023 — We found that NASA can help the whole-of-government UAP effort through...</span></span></span>

## How the Lesson Applies to Hacked Files

The McKinnon case provides a useful example of the evidence problem. McKinnon openly stated that he searched NASA and military systems for UFO-related information and later described seeing an image of a cigar-shaped object as well as references to "non-terrestrial officers". However, the public does not possess the original image, the underlying files, or the supporting technical records needed for independent analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found21 Jun 2006 — &#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found. **Missing provenance.** There is no publicly available chain linking the alleged files from the source system to a preserved copy available for forensic examination.
