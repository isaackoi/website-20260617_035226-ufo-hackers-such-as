## Introduction

One of the most consequential allegations in the Gary McKinnon case was not the claim that he searched military systems for UFO-related information, but the assertion that his actions helped bring down the United States Army’s Military District of Washington (MDW) network. The alleged outage became a central argument for US prosecutors because it transformed the case from unauthorised access into a claim of operational disruption affecting a major military command network. Yet the precise details of what happened, how long the outage lasted, and what technical actions caused it have been described differently in various official and legal accounts. The result is a dispute in which the existence of a serious network failure is widely acknowledged, while the scale and mechanics of the outage remain debated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud...Gary McKinnon, of London, England, was indicted in Alexandri...</span></span></span>

## What Really Happened to the Army Network Outage?

The strongest official version of events appears in court records and extradition proceedings. According to the House of Lords summary of the US case, McKinnon gained access to Army computers associated with the Military District of Washington and deleted critical operating-system files from nine computers. Those deletions allegedly caused the entire MDW network—described as comprising more than 2,000 computers—to be shut down for approximately 24 hours, significantly disrupting governmental functions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mckinn 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentMckinnon V Government of The United States of America...30 Jul 2008 — The 97 computers the appellant accessed were: 53 army...</span></span></span>

US Department of Justice statements were broadly consistent with that position. Prosecutors alleged that McKinnon accessed and damaged military systems, and later public statements connected his conduct with rendering the Military District of Washington network inoperable. The alleged damage formed part of the justification for criminal charges and extradition efforts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud...Gary McKinnon, of London, England, was indicted in Alexandri...</span></span></span>

What is notable is that the outage claim was not presented as a minor inconvenience. Prosecutors characterised it as the disabling of a major Army network used in support of national defence and security functions. That elevated the incident from a conventional hacking case into a matter with national-security implications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mckinn 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentMckinnon V Government of The United States of America...30 Jul 2008 — The 97 computers the appellant accessed were: 53 army...</span></span></span>

## Competing Official Timelines

While official sources generally agree that a serious outage occurred, descriptions of the event are not identical.

The most frequently cited version states that the MDW network was disabled for 24 hours after operating-system files were deleted from nine computers. This account appears in the House of Lords judgment summarising the American allegations and was repeated in later reporting on the extradition case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mckinn 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentMckinnon V Government of The United States of America...30 Jul 2008 — The 97 computers the appellant accessed were: 53 army...</span></span></span>

Other descriptions focus less on the deletion of specific files and more on the broader collapse of connectivity. During extradition proceedings, prosecutors argued that McKinnon's activities shut down internet access for roughly 2,000 military computers in the Washington area for a day. That framing emphasised the operational result rather than the exact technical mechanism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/pentagon-hacker-mckinnon-fights-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pinsentmasons.com">[Pinsent Masons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pinsentmasons.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pentagon hacker mckinnon fights extradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pinsent Masons&#x27;Pentagon hacker&#x27; McKinnon fights extradition28 Jul 2005 — Mark Summers, representing the US government, said McKinnon moun...</span></span></span>

These differences do not necessarily contradict one another. A network outage can be described either by its cause (deleted system files) or by its effect (loss of network services). However, they illustrate an important feature of the case: public accounts often relied on summaries of legal allegations rather than detailed technical incident reports. No publicly released forensic report has become as well known as the legal claims themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mckinn 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentMckinnon V Government of The United States of America...30 Jul 2008 — The 97 computers the appellant accessed were: 53 army...</span></span></span>

## Deleted Files and Network Failure

The key technical allegation was that critical operating-system files were removed from Army computers connected to the MDW network. According to the prosecution narrative, deleting those files rendered affected systems unusable and triggered wider network disruption. The House of Lords summary specifically referred to nine computers from which such files were deleted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mckinn 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentMckinnon V Government of The United States of America...30 Jul 2008 — The 97 computers the appellant accessed were: 53 army...</span></span></span>

Separate allegations in the case involved the deletion of user accounts and administrative data on other Army systems. Prosecutors claimed that 2,455 user accounts on an Army computer controlling network access were affected, causing systems to reboot and become inoperable. These allegations were distinct from, but related to, the broader narrative of disruption across military networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mckinn 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentMckinnon V Government of The United States of America...30 Jul 2008 — The 97 computers the appellant accessed were: 53 army...</span></span></span>

McKinnon, however, disputed claims that he intentionally caused major damage. Throughout the public controversy surrounding the case, he acknowledged obtaining unauthorised access but challenged the portrayal of himself as a malicious actor. He consistently maintained that his primary interest was searching for information about UFOs, advanced aerospace projects and related subjects rather than sabotaging military operations. Reports covering the extradition battle repeatedly noted his denial that he deliberately caused the level of damage alleged by US authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2012/oct/16/gary-mckinnon-timeline-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gary mckinnon timeline extradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianGary McKinnon timeline: events leading up to extradition...16 Oct 2012 — US prosecutors also allege he deleted files that sh...</span></span></span>

## Why the Outage Mattered

The Military District of Washington is not merely an administrative office network. It supports Army functions in the Washington, DC area, including installations connected to military command and support activities.
